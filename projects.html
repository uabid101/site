6-DOF Rigid Multibody Dynamics Simulator

May 2026 – Aug 2026 · Individual project · Python, NumPy, RK4, quaternions, pytest

Context

A numerical simulator of a rigid body with six degrees of freedom (three translations, three rotations), built on my own ahead of an end-of-studies internship in aerospace numerical simulation. The goal was to cover the whole technical chain expected in that kind of role: physical modelling, validated time integration, realistic mechanical joints, event sequencing and formal testing.

Method. The state of the body is its position, velocity, an orientation quaternion and its angular velocity. The dynamics follow the Newton–Euler equations, including the gyroscopic coupling ω × (Iω). Time integration uses a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme, written and validated on its own (exponential decay) before being applied to the full system. Joints are modelled in two ways: ball and pin joints with Lagrange multipliers and Baumgarte stabilization, and the slider joint with a penalty method (spring–damper restoring force). An event sequencer switches joints on or changes the applied forces based on a time or state condition.

Challenge

Writing the equations of motion is the easy part. The real difficulty is keeping the joints numerically robust once they are coupled to an explicit integrator, over thousands of time steps.

That forces a choice between two families of methods. Lagrange multipliers enforce a constraint almost exactly, but are sensitive to any inconsistency in the state. The penalty method is always bounded and robust, but only approximately satisfies the constraint. Rather than picking one by default, the project compares both explicitly and uses each where it fits best.

Validation. Reusable checks on physical invariants (energy, linear and angular momentum) and non-regression tests comparing each run against reference snapshots, all automated with pytest.

Analysis

Quaternion drift. Integrating dq/dt = ½ q ⊗ [0, ω] only preserves the quaternion norm to first order. The error builds up over thousands of RK4 steps and slowly corrupts the rotation, so the quaternion is now renormalized after every step.

Inconsistent initial states. Lagrange multipliers only correct the constraint acceleration, not a position or velocity error that is already there. An inconsistent start produced unbounded drift whatever the integrator. Scenarios are therefore built with consistent initial conditions (r₀ = P₀ − R(q)·d, v₀ = −ω × R(q)·d), and Baumgarte stabilization (g̈ + 2αġ + β²g = 0) was tuned and validated for time steps from 0.001 to 0.005 s.

Slider singularity. With Lagrange multipliers, the slider’s constraint Jacobians (built from a cross product between a body axis and its initial reference) vanish exactly at perfect alignment, which is the initial state. This diagnosis justified switching that joint to the penalty method.

Switching a joint on mid-motion. Activating a Lagrange constraint on a body already moving freely caused a delayed blow-up: the incompatible angular velocity was amplified by Baumgarte until overflow after 1 to 1.5 s of apparent stability. The demo uses the penalty method instead, and the limit is documented with possible fixes (velocity projection at activation, a dedicated constrained integrator).

The main takeaway is methodological: Lagrange where accuracy matters, penalty where robustness matters, and every limit documented rather than silently worked around.

GPU Chip Cooling via Microchannels

Jul 2025 – Aug 2025 · Individual project · Python, NumPy/SciPy, FEniCSx, scikit-learn, XGBoost

Context

A data-centre GPU such as the H100 dissipates 700 W over about 800 mm², a heat flux of roughly 875 kW/m², far beyond what a classic finned heat sink can remove. The goal was to design and numerically validate a liquid cooling system based on microchannels.

Method. The project couples three approaches in five steps:

  1. Solver validation: Poiseuille velocity profile against the analytical solution (error < 0.01%).
  2. 2D thermal CFD by finite differences: coupled convection–diffusion, explicit scheme marching along the channel.
  3. Parametric sweep over channel height, pressure drop and number of channels to find a viable operating point.
  4. Thermomechanical FEM in FEniCSx: heterogeneous Si/Cu conduction and von Mises stress.
  5. Machine-learning surrogate (Random Forest, XGBoost) trained on 79 validated CFD runs.

Challenge

Two constraints pull against each other: the wall temperature must stay below 85 °C, while the flow must remain laminar (Re < 2000), which is the validity condition of the physical model. Pushing more flow to cool better quickly breaks the second condition.

A single channel turned out to be physically insufficient, and the intuitive fix of shrinking it made things worse: at constant pressure drop, a smaller cross-section means less flow and more overheating.

On the structural side, silicon (2.6 ppm/K) and copper (16.5 ppm/K) expand very differently. Bonded together, this six-fold mismatch creates internal stresses that must stay well below the strength of silicon.

Analysis

From one channel to 112. Digging into the single-channel limit, instead of ignoring it, is what led to the multi-channel design: 112 channels in parallel keep each flow laminar while removing the full heat flux, with a wall at 62 °C. The simulated Nusselt number is 7.6% below the fully developed theoretical value, consistent with a channel where the thermal entrance region still plays a role.

Debugging the CFD. Most of the effort went into making the solver trustworthy: a sign error on the pressure gradient, a wrong hydraulic diameter formula, a W/m² vs W confusion in the energy balance, and wall temperatures blowing up when shrinking the channel because a stability criterion was violated. The step size is now computed automatically with a ×5 safety margin, and a Reynolds filter keeps every dataset point in the laminar regime.

FEM. The stress level (47 MPa, safety factor of 6.4) leaves a comfortable margin. Getting there meant separating the thermal load (linear form) from the stiffness (bilinear form) in FEniCSx, catching a typo in the Lamé law that produced stresses of 10¹² MPa, and adapting to API changes in dolfinx 0.11.

ML, read with caution. XGBoost reaches R² = 1.00 on wall temperature, but the dataset comes from a regular parametric grid, which makes the patterns unusually easy; noisier real data would give a more realistic 0.97 to 0.99. Both models only reach R² ≈ 0.78 on the mean Nusselt number, and a neural network failed (R² < 0.31) with only 79 points. The dataset is also thin at large channel heights, where most cases left the laminar regime; this is documented as a known limit rather than filtered away.
